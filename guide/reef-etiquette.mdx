Coral reefs are living ecosystems built by small animals called coral polyps. They support diverse marine life and attract visitors from around the world, but they can be damaged by direct contact, loose equipment, careless anchoring, pollution, and accumulated pressure from tourism. A responsible reef visit begins with a simple understanding: coral is not a rock, a resting place, or a souvenir.

## Responsible Reef Visits

### Choose a Responsible Operator

Before booking, choose an operator that explains local protected-area rules and keeps groups within conditions suitable for all participants. A reputable operator will brief guests on reef etiquette, enforce no-contact rules, and limit group sizes to reduce cumulative impact. If an operator does not mention local regulations or reef guidelines during the booking process, that is worth asking about directly.

### Prepare Before Entering the Water

<Steps>
  <Step title="Secure all loose equipment">
    Before entering the water, secure gauges, cameras, hoses, and any other gear so nothing can drag or swing freely across the reef. Unsecured equipment is one of the most common causes of accidental coral damage.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Practice buoyancy and fin control away from coral">
    If you are new to snorkeling or diving, practice buoyancy and fin control in open water before approaching the reef. Kicking or sinking unexpectedly near coral causes real damage even when unintentional.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Review the site briefing carefully">
    Listen to the full site briefing from the operator. Pay attention to restricted areas, mooring protocols, wildlife rules, and emergency procedures specific to that location.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  **Never stand, kneel, or hold onto living coral — even briefly.** A single touch can damage the thin layer of tissue that keeps a coral colony alive, and stressed coral does not recover quickly. Do not chase, feed, touch, or crowd marine wildlife for a photograph. Give all animals space and observe from a respectful distance without altering their natural behavior.
</Warning>

### Leave the Site as You Found It

Resist the impulse to collect shells, coral fragments, sand, plants, or animals, even small ones that appear loose. Unless local rules explicitly permit collection, everything on and around the reef should stay where it is. The goal is straightforward: leave the site with the same wildlife and structure that made it worth visiting.

Carry reusable water containers to reduce plastic waste in and around the water. Keep all trash secured throughout the trip and pack everything out, including packaging from snacks and equipment. Report pollution or reef damage through the appropriate local channel — the operator, park authority, or sanctuary office.

## Coastal Sun Protection

Sun exposure adds up quickly near the coast, and reef destinations often have specific rules about how sun protection products can be used in and around protected marine areas.

<Tip>
  **Wear a rash guard or long-sleeved sun shirt as your first line of reef-friendly sun protection.** NOAA recommends a reef-friendly approach to sun protection at coral reef destinations, and protective clothing such as a rash guard can significantly reduce the amount of exposed skin that requires sunscreen. Less sunscreen in the water means less potential impact on coral — and better protection for you throughout the day.
</Tip>

<Accordion title="Full Sun Protection Strategy for Reef Destinations">
  Plan sun protection as a layered combination of strategies rather than relying on a single product. The National Park Service recommends covering up, wearing a hat and sunglasses, seeking shade during the strongest part of the day, and applying appropriate sunscreen.

  **Clothing first:**
  A wide-brimmed hat, UV-blocking sunglasses, and a long-sleeved rash guard or sun shirt cover the most exposed areas and require no reapplication after swimming.

  **Sunscreen as a supplement:**
  Apply sunscreen to areas not covered by clothing and follow the product directions for reapplication, especially after swimming or toweling off. NOAA notes that some sunscreen ingredients can harm corals. Check current local regulations and sanctuary-specific guidance for the exact destination being visited — product regulations differ by sanctuary, park, island, and country. Do not rely solely on unregulated marketing labels such as "reef safe."

  **Shade and scheduling:**
  Build breaks out of direct sun into the day's schedule. Plan snorkeling and water activities for the morning or late afternoon when direct sun intensity is lower where possible.

  **Hydration:**
  Bring more drinking water than expected. Heat, physical activity, and limited shade on the water can change how someone feels over the course of the day. If anyone becomes unwell, move to a safer environment and seek appropriate help rather than continuing the activity.

  **A note on individual health:**
  Questions about sunscreen sensitivity, use on children, medication interactions, or skin conditions belong with a qualified medical professional. This guide provides general planning information only.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Destination-Specific Sunscreen Regulations">
  Some reef destinations have enacted legal restrictions on specific sunscreen ingredients. Regulations and permitted-product lists change over time and differ significantly by location.

  Before your trip, check the current requirements for the specific sanctuary, national park, island, or country you plan to visit. Relevant sources include the destination's official government or park authority website, the marine sanctuary or protected-area management office, and your dive or snorkel operator, who should be familiar with current local rules.

  Do not assume that a product marketed as "reef friendly" or "reef safe" meets the legal requirements of any particular destination — those labels are not regulated in most markets.
</Accordion>
