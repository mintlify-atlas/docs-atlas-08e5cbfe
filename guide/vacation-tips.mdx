A well-planned coastal vacation is more comfortable, more flexible, and easier to enjoy when things change — and things always change. The guidance below covers what to pack, how to schedule activities, what to review before a dive, how to prepare a basic first aid kit, and how to build sandcastles without creating hazards. All safety guidance is drawn from the National Park Service, U.S. Coast Guard, and NOAA.

---

## Beach Bag Checklist

<Accordion title="Everyday Essentials">
  Start with the basics that every member of the group will rely on throughout the day:

  - **Drinking water** — more than you expect to need
  - **Sunscreen** — appropriate for the destination; see reef-specific rules if applicable
  - **Protective clothing** — long sleeves, rash guard, or cover-up
  - **Wide-brimmed hat** and **UV-blocking sunglasses**
  - **Towels**
  - **Personal medications**

  Add **water-resistant footwear** if the beach has rocks, shells, hot boardwalks, or uneven access paths.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Valuables and Documents">
  Use a **waterproof pouch** for the items that cannot get wet or lost:

  - Phone
  - Identification
  - Payment card
  - Keys
  - Emergency contact information

  A separate **reusable bag for wet clothing** keeps the rest of the bag dry. Bring a dedicated **trash bag** so packing out wrappers and waste is easy throughout the day.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Food and Drink">
  If food is part of the plan, store it securely to prevent spillage or wildlife access. Follow all local rules about glass containers — many beaches prohibit them for safety reasons. Keep waste sealed and pack it out completely.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Families with Children">
  Families may also need:

  - **Properly fitted flotation devices** — sized for each child, not one-size-fits-all
  - **Shade** — a pop-up tent, umbrella, or canopy (check beach rules before bringing)
  - **Extra clothing** for weather changes or unexpected soaking
  - **Quiet activities** for breaks out of the sun

  Bring only toys that can be controlled in expected wind. Inflatable toys and lightweight objects can blow away quickly and **should never be treated as safety equipment**.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Check Destination Rules Before Packing">
  Destination websites often list restrictions that are easy to miss. Some beaches prohibit or limit:

  - Umbrellas and shade tents
  - Pets
  - Open fires
  - Fishing gear
  - Flotation devices
  - Wheeled equipment

  Accessibility information can also determine whether a beach mat, mobility aid, or alternate access point is needed. Packing from verified local information prevents bringing the wrong equipment — or forgetting something essential.
</Accordion>

## Planning Coastal Activities

Coastal vacations work best when the schedule respects weather, tides, travel time, and the group's energy.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Start with activities that require reservations">
    Book guided kayaking, snorkeling, diving, and similar activities first. Confirm age, swimming ability, certification, health, and equipment requirements directly with the operator before paying.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Match every activity to the least experienced participant">
    A route described as easy may still involve heat, waves, current, long sun exposure, or limited access to restrooms and drinking water. Ask the operator specific questions about duration, physical demands, shade, entry and exit conditions, and what happens if weather changes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Leave room between activities">
    Time is needed for transportation, equipment fitting, meals, rest, and unexpected delays. A flexible afternoon can become a beach walk, museum visit, scenic drive, or relaxed meal if wind or storms cancel a water activity.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Keep all confirmation details together">
    Store confirmation numbers, meeting locations, cancellation policies, and emergency contacts in one place that all adults in the group can access.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Recheck conditions on the day of the activity">
    Do not rely on information saved earlier in the trip. Recheck official conditions and any weather updates on the morning of each activity before heading out.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Tip>
  A successful itinerary is not the one that fits the most activities. It is the one the group can complete comfortably, safely, and with energy left to enjoy the trip.
</Tip>

## Diving Checklist

Scuba diving requires training, suitable health, maintained equipment, and planning specific to the dive site. This checklist is not a substitute for certification or professional instruction — use it to organize questions for a qualified operator and follow the standards of your certifying organization.

<Accordion title="Confirm Certification and Health Readiness">
  Before the dive, verify that your certification and recent experience match the planned conditions:

  - Depth and environment (open water, drift, wreck, cave)
  - Current, visibility, and water temperature
  - Entry and exit method (shore, boat, ladder, giant stride)

  Tell the operator about any relevant **health changes, current medications, anxiety, or a long break from diving**. A refresher session may be appropriate before an unfamiliar or demanding dive.
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Full Equipment Review with Your Buddy">
  Review the complete setup with your assigned buddy before entering the water:

  - Air supply (pressure, valve open, breathing freely)
  - Releases and buckles on BCD and harness
  - Weights and weight-release mechanism
  - Buoyancy controls (inflate, deflate, dump valves)
  - Gauges and dive computer
  - Alternate air source (location, accessible to buddy)
  - Exposure protection (wetsuit or drysuit — zips, seals, hood, gloves)
  - Signaling devices (surface marker buoy, whistle, light)
  - Any operator-required equipment
</Accordion>

<Accordion title="Pre-Dive Briefing Points">
  Discuss the following with your buddy and dive leader before the dive:

  - Planned route and maximum depth and time
  - Hand signals and communication plan
  - Separation procedure (what to do if you lose visual contact)
  - Turn pressure (the tank pressure at which the dive ends)
  - Entry and exit plan
  - Emergency plan (nearest emergency contact, DAN number if applicable)

  Anyone should be able to end a dive if something does not feel right — there is no need to justify a conservative decision.
</Accordion>

## First Aid Preparation

<Steps>
  <Step title="Research the destination before the trip">
    Identify the nearest lifeguard station, ranger contact, urgent care location, and local emergency number before you travel. Remote beaches may have limited cellular service and longer response times. Share this information with everyone in the group.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pack appropriate supplies in a waterproof container">
    Carry supplies appropriate for minor problems you are trained to handle:

    - Adhesive bandages in assorted sizes
    - Sterile gauze pads and rolls
    - Medical tape
    - Disposable gloves
    - Tweezers
    - Basic wound-cleaning supplies
    - Personal medications and any emergency items prescribed for members of the group

    Store everything in a **waterproof container** and replace expired or damaged items before the trip.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Consider a first aid and CPR course">
    Training matters more than an oversized kit. A recognized first aid and CPR course helps someone assess a situation, protect themselves, call for help, and provide appropriate care while responders are on the way. Do not use unfamiliar medications or attempt procedures beyond your training.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Warning>
  **For any serious emergency, contact emergency services immediately and follow dispatcher instructions.**

  Situations that require an immediate emergency call include:
  - Serious or uncontrolled bleeding
  - Difficulty breathing or no breathing
  - Loss of consciousness
  - Suspected spinal injury
  - Severe allergic reaction
  - Drowning or near-drowning
  - Heat illness (heat exhaustion or heat stroke)

  Alert a lifeguard or ranger when one is available. This guide supports trip preparation and does not provide diagnosis or individualized medical treatment.
</Warning>

## Safer Sandcastles

Sandcastle building is a relaxed family activity — but deep holes and unstable tunnels can collapse quickly and cause serious injury.

<Warning>
  **Do not dig holes deeper than knee level.** The National Park Service warns that sand collapses easily and can bury a person with little warning. Local parks may have additional rules about digging depth, permitted tools, protected dune areas, and where structures may be built. Check the specific beach's rules before digging.
</Warning>

<Steps>
  <Step title="Choose a safe location">
    Select a flat area away from dune vegetation, wildlife nesting areas, access paths, emergency vehicle routes, and the reach of rising water.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Build outward, not downward">
    Use small hand tools rather than large shovels. Build walls, towers, and surface details outward rather than excavating a deep pit or tunnel.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Supervise children closely">
    Keep an eye on the tide and waves. A structure that begins far from the waterline may sit in the surf zone as the tide rises, where children can be knocked over or cut off from adults.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Fill every hole before leaving">
    Before the group leaves, flatten all structures and completely fill every hole. Unfilled holes can trip other visitors, interfere with patrol vehicles, and create obstacles for wildlife. Pack out all molds, broken toys, string, and decorations.
  </Step>
</Steps>
