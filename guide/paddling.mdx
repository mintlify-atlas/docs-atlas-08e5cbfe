A kayak may look simple, but a safe outing still depends on equipment, weather, route selection, and an honest assessment of everyone's experience. Taking a few extra minutes before launch — checking gear, briefing the group, and leaving a float plan — is the most effective preparation a paddler can do.

## Before You Paddle

Work through these steps each time you go out, regardless of how familiar the route may seem.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Choose a route that fits your group">
    Choose protected water and a manageable distance when someone is new to paddling. Ocean, river, and large-lake conditions can change quickly. Unfamiliar routes are better explored with a qualified local guide who knows the tides, currents, and local hazards.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Wear and fit your life jacket">
    Wear a properly fitted, U.S. Coast Guard-approved life jacket that is appropriate for the activity. A life jacket only works if it is on and adjusted correctly before you are in the water.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Inspect all equipment before launching">
    Check the kayak, seat, paddle, foot supports, drain plug, and any attached gear before entering the water. A missing drain plug or loose foot support discovered mid-trip is far harder to address than one found at the launch site.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Secure supplies in waterproof storage">
    Stow drinking water, sun protection, a whistle, and a reliable communication device in waterproof storage attached to the kayak. Loose items can be lost if the kayak capsizes.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Dress for water temperature, not just air temperature">
    Dress for the water temperature as well as the air temperature. An unexpected capsize changes what the body is exposed to immediately. Cold water can cause incapacitation quickly even on a warm day.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Check the weather forecast">
    The U.S. Coast Guard recommends checking weather before departure, knowing the route and how to return, and confirming that all safety equipment is functional. Do not rely on yesterday's forecast — check current conditions on the morning you plan to paddle.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Leave a float plan with a responsible person">
    Before launching, give a float plan to someone staying ashore who can act if the group does not return.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Note>
  **What a complete float plan should include:**

  - Who is going (names and contact information)
  - Launch point and planned route
  - Expected return time
  - Equipment on board (kayak description, life jackets, communication devices)
  - What to do — and who to call — if the group does not return by the agreed time

  Leave this information with a trusted person who will actually follow through if the deadline passes. A float plan is only useful if someone will act on it.
</Note>

## On the Water

Preparation continues once you are underway. Conditions on the water can shift faster than they appear to from the shore.

<CardGroup cols={2}>
  <Card title="Stay in Communication Range" icon="radio" href="https://www.uscgboating.org/">
    Stay close enough to the group to communicate by voice or signal at all times. If someone falls behind or the group splits, wait and regroup before continuing.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Monitor Sky and Water" icon="cloud-sun" href="https://www.uscgboating.org/">
    Watch for building clouds, whitecaps, changes in wave direction, or unusual currents. Changes in the sky are often visible well before the weather reaches your location.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Turn Around Before You Are Tired" icon="rotate-left" href="https://www.uscgboating.org/">
    Fatigue affects paddling control, judgment, and the ability to self-rescue. Plan the turnaround point for before — not after — the group starts to tire.
  </Card>
  <Card title="Shorten the Trip If Conditions Change" icon="route" href="https://www.uscgboating.org/assets/1/AssetManager/Boaters-Guide-to-Federal-Requirements-for-Receational-Boats-20231108.pdf">
    If the route, weather, or group's energy changes unexpectedly, shorten the trip. The original destination is never worth compromising the return.
  </Card>
</CardGroup>

<Warning>
  **Wind that helps on the way out can make the return much harder.** A tailwind that pushes the group comfortably outward becomes a headwind on the paddle back. Factor wind direction and expected changes into your route planning before you launch, and be prepared to cut the trip short if conditions build. As the U.S. Coast Guard guidance emphasizes: reaching the original destination is never more important than returning safely.
</Warning>
